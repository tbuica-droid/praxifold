# From a first AI challenge to independent practice

Curriculum audit for Praxifold · 25 November 2026

**Praxifold currently teaches useful AI judgment and introductory evaluation. It has yet to establish that a learner can independently build, operate or research an AI system.** The largest gap is ownership. Today the platform supplies the problem, evidence, controls and scoring rules. Advanced learners should increasingly choose those things themselves, then defend their choices against an unfamiliar case.

A defensible destination is **broad AI fluency plus independently demonstrated expertise in a named specialty**. Someone might be excellent at evaluating document assistants while still learning robot control. A portfolio should tell a reader exactly which claim the evidence supports. For the founder, the first milestone would be a project you can start with an empty workspace, rerun a week later and explain to someone who challenges your assumptions.

## How I checked coverage

I compared the implemented question bank, drills, lab controls, grading and portfolio behavior with three kinds of evidence. The ACM/IEEE-CS/AAAI curriculum provides a breadth check. University assignments show how that breadth translates into technical work. Research papers, institutional guidance and protocol documentation supply evidence for particular design and evaluation decisions. The ordering, example projects and proposed grading rules below are my curriculum design recommendations. This is a purposive curriculum review, not an empirical evaluation of Praxifold graduates. [CS2023 curriculum report](https://csed.acm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/CS2023-Report.htm) · [Berkeley CS188 projects](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs188/archive/fa25/projects/) · [Stanford ML systems design](https://stanford-cs329s.github.io/index.html)

| What exists today | What a learner actually does | Limit of the evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nine placement questions | Makes decisions about sources, instructions, data, workflows, approval and evaluation. | Three items per path give a starting recommendation. The learner has yet to implement anything. |
| D1–D6 and the launch lab | Checks claims, rewrites a brief, recomputes rates, revises a chart and submits a cited decision. | The app checks selected facts, calculations and references. Whether the recommendation persuades an appropriate audience requires judgment. |
| D7–D10 and the briefing lab | Selects authoritative evidence, changes rules, inspects traces and triggers valid completion or a review pause. | These are bounded replays. The learner does not write a service, integrate a new API or operate a deployed system. |
| D11–D12 and the classifier lab | Spots leakage, trains a supplied decision stump, compares its held-out score with a baseline and inspects errors. | Real computation on one synthetic split. The learner has yet to acquire data, implement training or quantify uncertainty across datasets. |
| Attendance capstone, review and portfolio | Applies evidence to a new brief, records self-review and exports work. | Fresh context is valuable. Stored answers and objective checks support narrower claims than independent expert review. |

## Competency map and missing work

Each row describes a **proposed project or assessment**, including the rows with playable foundations. “Introduced” means the beta exercises part of the competency. “Gap” means a relevant scored performance is missing. The three existing entry paths can stay. These competencies cut across them.

| Competency | Beta coverage | Work a learner should be able to submit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Frame a useful problem | Introduced in D1–D3 and launch | Interview a stakeholder, define an observable outcome, price two different errors and compare with a human, rule or spreadsheet baseline. Explain when the baseline is sufficient. |
| 2. Program and reason quantitatively | Arithmetic introduced in D4. Coding and mathematical foundations are gaps. | Load and join unfamiliar files with SQL or Python, write tests, use Git and reproduce an environment. Use vectors for similarity, conditional probability for a decision, and a loss curve to explain a gradient update. Offer an optional bridge with a performance-based skip. |
| 3. Collect and govern data | Cohort errors and source dates introduced | Produce a versioned dataset and datasheet. Document permission, collection, sampling, labeling disagreements, missingness, duplicate identities, retention and excluded uses. Choose the split unit before touching the test set. |
| 4. Search, reason and plan | Gap | Solve a scheduling or routing problem with constraints and graph search. Compare optimality and runtime with a greedy rule. Extend it to uncertain observations and identify where probabilistic reasoning changes the decision. |
| 5. Learn from data | One supplied classifier | Implement regression and classification, compare a linear model with a tree, tune using validation data, and examine regularization and imbalance. Use clustering or dimensionality reduction on unlabeled records and explain what would make the grouping useful. |
| 6. Design experiments and evaluate claims | Introduced in D1, D11, D12 and capstone | Freeze a baseline and metric, estimate uncertainty, check subgroup errors and calibration, and separate correlation from causal evidence. Design a randomized comparison or explain confounding. Report a decision that changes when error costs change. |
| 7. Understand neural networks | Gap | Implement a small network and backpropagation, check a gradient numerically, compare training and validation curves, and isolate a failed run. Account for compute, memory and reproducibility. |
| 8. Understand and adapt language models | Prompt examples introduced. Model construction is a gap. | Build a tokenizer and tiny causal language model. Trace attention and sampling, distinguish pretraining, inference and post-training, then compare prompting, retrieval and fine-tuning under matched constraints. Document data rights and limits of the small-scale experiment. |
| 9. Work across modalities | Chart revision introduced. Learned perception is a gap. | Compare image, text and joint inputs on a document task. Inspect OCR or layout errors, corrupted images and accessibility. Evaluate audio on different recording conditions. In a generative project, test controllability, provenance and audience judgment as well as output quality. |
| 10. Design for people and domain outcomes | Decision memos and review gates introduced | Observe a person attempting a real task. Build a correction or opt-out interaction, make uncertainty intelligible, and test whether people catch consequential errors. Compare time and correctness with their original process. |
| 11. Retrieve and ground answers | Source authority introduced in D7 and briefing replay | Build keyword and embedding retrieval baselines on the same corpus, then test hybrid search or reranking. Measure whether retrieval found the right passage separately from whether the final answer is supported. Include an unanswerable question and a superseded policy. |
| 12. Integrate tools and coordinate work | Decisions and replays introduced. Independent implementation is a gap. | Define a typed interface, validate inputs and outputs, separate permissions, and recover from timeouts and duplicate deliveries. Compare a direct function, fixed workflow and bounded agent. Add an MCP adapter only when another compatible client needs the capability. |
| 13. Serve and operate a system | Gap | Move a reproducible batch job into an API or keep it batch with reasons. Set quality, latency and cost targets. Measure tail latency and throughput under load, track versions and distribution shift, then rehearse fallback and rollback. Compare caching, batching, quantization or a smaller model on measured trade-offs. |
| 14. Protect information and assign accountability | Approval introduced. Infrastructure and governance are gaps. | Draw the data flow, identify who can see prompts and outputs, restrict access, set retention, assign key ownership and rehearse recovery. Document fairness, affected groups, review responsibilities, incident escalation and grounds for stopping deployment. |
| 15. Learn by acting | Gap | In a simulator, compare a hand-designed policy and a learned policy. Explain reward, exploration, delayed consequences and evaluation variability across seeds. Show how a policy that earns more reward can still fail the intended task. |
| 16. Conduct and communicate research | Capability-claim drill introduces falsification | Reproduce a published result at a declared scale, vary one factor in an ablation, publish negative findings, and have a second person rerun the analysis. Distinguish a replication, an extension and a claim of novelty. |

## Two examples in plain English

**What does an MCP server do?** Imagine a library has a catalog that several AI applications need to search. An MCP server can offer a named tool such as “look up a book,” describe the inputs it accepts and return a structured result. The AI application hosts an MCP client that connects to the server. A server can also expose readable resources or reusable prompts. A workflow determines when a lookup happens. A harness supplies the environment, controls and checks around the work. These are related concepts with different jobs. [Model Context Protocol architecture](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/docs/learn/architecture) · [July 2026 specification release](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)

**Proposed exercise.** Start with one application and a local catalog function. Add a second client that needs the same capability. Have the learner justify keeping the function, exposing an ordinary API, or adopting MCP. For the MCP version, both clients must find the same book through a read-only tool. A missing book must yield a meaningful empty result, and an unauthorized request must reveal no private record. Grade the observable behavior and the integration trade-off. Knowing the acronym earns no project credit.

**What does “self-hosted encryption” mean for an AI project?** Usually the team has several decisions to make. It can run the model on its own infrastructure or send requests to a managed service. Separately, it must protect network traffic, stored records and access to keys. A managed service can support customer-managed keys. A self-hosted service can still write the full prompt to a readable log. In ordinary inference, the component processing the prompt needs usable data at processing time. Storage encryption alone says little about that component’s access. [NIST key-management guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/57/pt1/r5/final) · [NIST transport security guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/52/r2/final) · [Google Cloud’s concrete example of customer-managed keys](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kms/docs/cmek)

| Decision for the learner | Evidence to request |
| --- | --- |
| Who runs the model and patches it? | Compare a managed API, a model in the team’s cloud account and an on-device model. Name the operator, available hardware and support burden. |
| Where does readable data go? | Trace the prompt, retrieved records, output, logs, caches and backups. Mark each point where a person or service can read them. |
| Who controls the keys, and who can use them? | Compare provider-managed, customer-managed and externally held keys where supported. Identify identities allowed to decrypt, rotation ownership, recovery and the effect of losing access. |
| Which option meets the actual brief? | Use synthetic records to compare quality, latency, total cost, retention and recovery. Include labor and idle capacity in the self-hosted estimate. A missing contractual or operational fact stays an open question. |

**Proposed exercise.** A museum wants captions for a public collection and a separate assistant for confidential donor notes. Give the learner the same three deployment options, two budgets and different data-handling requirements. Ask for two decisions, a data-flow diagram and a recovery drill. Plausible answers can differ between collections. A reviewer should be able to trace each decision to an explicit requirement.

## Sequence and specialties

1. **Start with the current studio.** Use placement for routing. Finish a decision, a system repair and a model audit. A learner who already has code or research experience can attempt a fresh entry project directly.
2. **Build the independent data and programming bridge first.** Supply an unfamiliar, imperfect dataset, a short brief, an executable workspace and a hidden test set. The learner writes the analysis, fits a baseline and leaves a reproducible handoff. This unlocks meaningful assessment in almost every later branch.
3. **Add the shared technical core.** Pair classical search and probabilistic inference with supervised and unsupervised learning. Follow with neural networks, evaluation, human use and information handling. Require a fresh project that combines two methods and explains why one was selected.
4. **Develop one end-to-end specialty before expanding the catalog.** My first choice for Praxifold is applied AI systems because its existing briefing case can grow naturally from source judgment into retrieval, integration, deployment and evaluation. Keep distinct advanced routes for learners with different destinations.
5. **Offer independent review after a specialty has enough playable depth.** Give learners the brief, constraints and acceptance criteria. Let them choose the architecture. Change one requirement after submission and ask them to adapt and defend the result.

| Proposed specialty | Prerequisites | Example independent examination |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Applied AI and ML systems | Programming, data, evaluation, retrieval and operations | Ship a permission-aware document assistant. An evaluator changes a policy, removes a source and increases load. Demonstrate correct updates, abstention, monitoring and recovery. |
| Modeling and research | Linear algebra, probability, optimization, experimental design and neural networks | Replicate a small model result, isolate a claimed improvement and explain when it disappears. A reviewer reruns the work with another seed or dataset. |
| Human-centered AI and evaluation | Domain research, statistical measurement, interaction design and responsible use | Design a decision aid with domain users. Measure reliance, correction, subgroup effects and transfer to a different team. Defend a decision to launch, revise or stop. |
| Perception, language and multimodal systems | Neural networks, modality-specific data and evaluation | Build a document or speech system and report failures across layout, image quality, language or recording conditions. Compare modality-specific baselines. |
| Decision-making and robotics | Search, probability, control, simulation and reinforcement learning | Adapt a simulated agent to changed dynamics under a safety constraint. Compare it with a planner and explain the evidence still needed before any physical deployment. |

For later depth, branch into forecasting and time series, recommenders and ranking, causal inference, knowledge graphs, optimization, multi-agent coordination, interpretability, privacy-preserving learning or efficient inference. Admit a branch to the expert catalog when it has prerequisites, independent work, fresh evaluation data and a qualified reviewer. Maintaining named specialties makes the coverage claim inspectable as the field changes.

## What would justify saying the platform develops experts?

**Proposed evidence ladder.** Record guided completion, fresh unaided performance, and independently reviewed specialty work separately. For an advanced capstone, require a runnable artifact, data provenance, a baseline, an untouched test case, error analysis, operating constraints and a short defense. Design the adaptation task so copying a prior solution fails for a substantive reason. A learner who discovers that a simpler approach is sufficient can earn full credit. Transfer across contexts and reproducibility are the reasons for these design choices. [National Research Council, Learning and Transfer](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/9853/chapter/6) · [NeurIPS paper checklist](https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist)

**Proposed reviewer rule.** Reviewers rate problem framing, technical correctness, evaluation, responsible operation, and transfer as *needs guidance*, *independent* or *can defend trade-offs*. Require independent performance in all five dimensions and a defensible specialty decision. A missing baseline, contaminated test set or unresolved disclosure of restricted data returns the project for revision. Preserve both attempts and reviewer reasons. Calibrate reviewers on common examples, double-score a sample blind and adjudicate disagreements before awarding a credential.

**Validate the learning claim in a pilot.** Give entrants a fresh baseline task. At exit, give a matched-difficulty task in a different domain, then a delayed task several weeks later. Counterbalance task versions and conceal attempt order from reviewers. Report entrants, completers, learners attempting follow-up, independent passes and reviewer agreement. Show both the pass rate among everyone who entered and the rate among those assessed. Examine prior experience and dropouts. A pre/post gain is encouraging evidence of progress. A credible causal claim about the platform would additionally need a comparison group and attention to selection effects. Publish the actual specialty, tasks and limitations alongside any outcome claim.

**First release decision.** Fund the independent dataset-to-decision project, its scoring cases and reviewer calibration together. The practical acceptance test is whether a stranger can rerun a learner’s work and whether the learner can adapt it when the unit of analysis changes. That closes a larger gap in today’s product than another guided tour of an AI tool.
